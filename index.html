Abstract

Robots, and humanoid robots in particular, are increasingly competent at individual behaviors, each obtained by training a specialized controller. A specialized skill is quick to train, can be validated on its own and converges reliably because the problem it faces is narrow. None of this is true of a single end-to-end policy asked to cover everything. What remains fragile is the transition between specialized skills. We argue that the composition of independent sub-policies deserves to be treated as a research problem in its own right, rather than as an implementation detail left to whatever mechanism happens to be at hand.

Reliable composition is what turns a collection of separate skills into a repertoire that can be used, extended and shared. More fundamentally, if control can be passed between specialized policies safely and reliably, the choice of what the robot should do next can be delegated to a component of an entirely different nature, such as a planner, whose decisions can be verified before execution.

Problem Definition

Many tasks are made of regimes that can be learned separately: walking, jumping, kicking a ball. Each regime gets its own skill, or expert, πi, trained and validated on its own. The question is how to combine these experts into a single controller that solves the whole task as well as an end-to-end policy would, while keeping them independent and reusable.

Taxonomy of Skill Composition

We identify two parallel ways of composing experts, which differ in what the decision layer above them outputs. In bridging, a decision layer σ names one active expert at a time. In blending, a decision layer ω weights the contribution of several experts, and their actions are merged into a single command.

Bridging

Overview

Bridging is sequential composition: one expert controls the robot at a time, and the decision layer decides which. The difficulty lies in the handover. An expert only succeeds from the states it was trained on, its initiation set, so control can pass safely only if the robot is inside the incoming expert's set at the moment of the switch. Bridging is the problem of making that happen, and it can be solved in several ways:

  • Shape the outgoing expert so that it ends inside the incoming expert's initiation set, as in skill chaining.
  • Widen the incoming expert's initiation set, so that it accepts whatever the outgoing one leaves behind.
  • Choose the moment of the switch, waiting until the robot happens to be in a suitable state.
  • Insert a transition policy between the two experts, which steers the robot to the other expert's initiation set.

Existing approaches fall short:

  • Skill chaining assumes the outgoing skill runs to its natural end. Here the switch comes from outside, at an arbitrary moment, so nothing guarantees the robot is in a state the next skill can start from.
  • Methods that tune or widen the experts so they fit together give up their independence: adding a skill means revisiting the others.

Method

We bridge with a transition policy, called bridge, keeping every expert frozen. When the decision layer asks for a different expert, the bridge takes control immediately. Its only job is to leave the robot in a state the incoming expert can take over from, whatever state the outgoing one left behind. Once the handover is done, the bridge disappears.

Results

Humanoid: jumping while walking

A Unitree G1 alternates between a frozen walking skill and a frozen jumping skill trained from rest. The bridge carries it from mid stride into a crouch the jump can start from. The ghost shows the nominal jump, as performed from the states the skill was trained on.

Cart-pole: swing up to balance

A swing up skill pumps energy into the pole but cannot balance it; a balancer holds it upright but only works near vertical. Hand over too early or too late and the pole is lost. The bridge guides the system into a state from which the balancer can succeed.

Differential drive: cruise to tight turn

A fast straight line drive meets a tight turn that is safe only at low speed and rolls the chassis if entered at cruise. The difficulty is speed, not timing: the bridge sheds exactly as much of it as the turn requires, bringing the robot into a state from which the turn can be executed safely.

Blending

Overview

Blending runs several experts at once and merges their outputs, weighted by a decision layer that gives each expert a share of the command. A change of regime is absorbed by the merge itself, so no extra mechanism is needed at the moment the experts' involvement changes. This suits tasks that are genuinely a superposition of skills, such as moving while manipulating or kicking.

Method

  • Two experts on a humanoid ball kicking task: a frozen locomotion policy that tracks velocity commands, and a kicking policy trained as a residual on top of it.
  • A learned orchestrator watches the robot and the ball and outputs blend weights, independently per joint.
  • The two Gaussian action distributions are merged as a weighted product, so the more confident expert dominates where it is weighted more.
  • The orchestrator starts almost entirely on locomotion, and kick strength and kick weight are penalized: the residual gains authority only where it earns it.
  • Only the kick and the orchestrator are trained; rewards are split into groups, each with its own critic.
  • No dedicated recovery skill: post kick states already fall within what the locomotion policy recovers from.

The orchestrator learns by itself to favor the kick only close to the ball, recovering the intuitive far/near behavior without it being imposed as a rule.

Results

Blended kick on a real humanoid

The robot walks towards the ball, adjusts its stride, kicks with the blended action and recovers back into locomotion, all without a hand designed switching rule.
